    Section 2 Course Overview

    Subsubsection 2.1 👋 Welcome!

    I’m your instructor, Steven Clontz. I prefer to be addressed as "Dr./Prof. Clontz" or "Dr./Prof. C", and use "he/him" pronouns.
    🔗
    My title here at South is "Professor of Mathematics", which means I’m a tenured research mathematician, responsible for teaching courses to students like you, as well as publishing papers that prove new mathematical facts. My scholarly interests include topology (the area of math that explains how you visually recognize different shaped fonts as the same letters), game theory (the area of math that explains how to win games as optimally as possible), sociotechnical infrastructure and human-computer interaction in mathematics (areas of computer science that study how humans use technology to collaborate on mathematical problems), and mathematics education and outreach (a.k.a. how to make math engaging and approachable by general audiences).
    🔗
    Fun fact: I’m a huge Nintendo 🎮 and Disney 🏰 nerd. My eight-year-old got As in math last year, but I’m secretly more excited that she’s gotten deep into the lore of Deltarune (not the Weird Route, obvs). If you need to distract me, get me talking about Super Nintendo World, which I finally got to visit last summer (but failed to beat any minigames).
    🔗
    If you are interested in any combination of math, games, puzzles, computers, and/or education, I’m always interested in finding ways to support student projects. Just let me know!
    🔗

    Subsubsection 2.2 🧮 What are we learning together this semester?

    Our course this semester is an introduction to linear algebra. This branch of mathematics is focused on vector spaces (collections of vectors for which addition and multiplication is defined) and linear maps (rules for transforming vector spaces that preserve addition and multiplication).
    🔗
    Linear algebra provides the tools for converting many kinds of data from one format to another without losing its linear structure. For example, linear algebra explains how to transform a 3D model into a 2D image in a sensible way:
    🔗
    This course will be structured around five big questions, each of which is split into 4 to 7 learning outcomes. Don’t worry if you don’t know what all these words mean just yet - we have a semester to get it all figured out.
    🔗

    Note 2.3. 💡 Big Questions and Learning Outcomes.

    • How can we solve systems of linear equations? By the end of this course, you should be able to...
      • Translate back and forth between a system of linear equations, a vector equation, and the corresponding augmented matrix.
        🔗
        🔗
      • Explain why a matrix isn’t in reduced row echelon form, and put a matrix in reduced row echelon form.
        🔗
        🔗
      • Determine the number of solutions for a system of linear equations or a vector equation.
        🔗
        🔗
      • Compute the solution set for a system of linear equations or a vector equation with infinitely many solutions.
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    • What is a space of Euclidean vectors? By the end of this course, you should be able to...
      • Determine if a Euclidean vector can be written as a linear combination of a given set of Euclidean vectors by solving an appropriate vector equation.
        🔗
        🔗
      • Determine if a set of Euclidean vectors spans Rn by solving appropriate vector equations.
        🔗
        🔗
      • Determine if a subset of Rn is a subspace or not.
        🔗
        🔗
      • Determine if a set of Euclidean vectors is linearly dependent or independent by solving an appropriate vector equation.
        🔗
        🔗
      • Explain why a set of Euclidean vectors is or is not a basis of Rn.
        🔗
        🔗
      • Compute a basis for the subspace spanned by a given set of Euclidean vectors, and determine the dimension of the subspace.
        🔗
        🔗
      • Find a basis for the solution set of a homogeneous system of equations.
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    • How can we understand linear maps algebraically? By the end of this course, you should be able to...
      • Determine if a map between Euclidean vector spaces is linear or not.
        🔗
        🔗
      • Translate back and forth between a linear transformation of Euclidean spaces and its standard matrix, and perform related computations.
        🔗
        🔗
      • Compute a basis for the kernel and a basis for the image of a linear map, and verify that the rank-nullity theorem holds for a given linear map.
        🔗
        🔗
      • Determine if a given linear map is injective and/or surjective.
        🔗
        🔗
      • Explain why a given set with defined addition and scalar multiplication does satisfy a given vector space property, but nonetheless isn’t a vector space.
        🔗
        🔗
      • Answer questions about vector spaces of polynomials or matrices.
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    • What algebraic structure do matrices have? By the end of this course, you should be able to...
      • Multiply matrices.
        🔗
        🔗
      • Determine if a matrix is invertible, and if so, compute its inverse and use it to solve an appropriate system of equations.
        🔗
        🔗
      • Calculate the change-of-basis matrix for the standard basis to a non-standard basis of Euclidean space.
        🔗
        🔗
      • Express row operations through matrix multiplication.
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    • How do we understand linear maps geometrically? By the end of this course, you should be able to...
      • Describe how a row operation affects the determinant of a matrix.
        🔗
        🔗
      • Compute the determinant of a matrix.
        🔗
        🔗
      • Find the eigenvalues of a matrix.
        🔗
        🔗
      • Find a basis for the eigenspace of a matrix associated with a given eigenvalue.
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗

    Subsubsection 2.4 💻 Technology

    I’m a huge advocate for using technology to help with learning and doing mathematics. The main tool I recommend for this class is SageCell, a website that runs code written in Python, SageMath, Octave, and more. <https://sagecell.sagemath.org>
    🔗
    Of course, what about AI? 🤖 That’s its own can of worms for so many reasons, but the short version is that the goal of this class is for you to learn math, not to learn how to pretend you know math by parroting what a computer tells you to say. I’m setting you up for success to learn things for yourself, so use AI tools sparingly, if at all.

    Subsubsection 2.5 👥 Working Together

    You’ll be grouped into teams early in the semester, and you will be working in teams during most of our time spent in our class meetings. But if you don’t like team projects, don’t worry: your letter grade in this course depends only upon your individual learning of the course material.
    🔗
    Before each new Big Question, you’ll complete an individual readiness quiz, followed by a team readiness quiz.
    🔗
    We’ll then explore each relevant Learning Outcome together in class over the course of three-ish weeks. I’ll introduce each topic and serve as your guide as you work through team activities that will help you understand each topic and prepare you to answer relevant questions.
    🔗

    Note 2.5. 🙌 Attendance.

    To ensure all teams are engaged in learning the course material, regular in-person attendance is necessary.
    🔗
    All students are allowed five class absences for any reason. After that a grade penalty will be applied, unless you meet with me during scheduled office hours to discuss the absences and make a plan for re-engagement in the course. There’s no official designation of "excused" or "unexcused" absences, but if your absences are "excusable" (sickness, interviews, university events) this will be a very quick and easy conversation!
    🔗
    🔗
    One important point: at the end of the day I need to see what you’ve learned. If you get help on a submission from a classmate or a technology like generative AI, make it clear that you did so in your submission, or you risk being liable for plagiarism and an academic dishonesty accusation. If you make it clear, I still need to have enough information to know that you personally understand the relevant learning outcome.
    🔗

    Subsubsection 2.7 📆 Due Dates and Deadlines

    Anything I assign for you to do in this class will be assigned in Canvas. Canvas allows me to set "Due Dates" and "Deadlines".
    🔗
    The Due Date marks when the assignment is positioned in your Canvas calendar, and any submission received after the Due Date is marked as "Late". There’s no penalty for being "Late"; however, my time to help students is finite, and if I have to make a choice, I’ll spend that time on the students with fewer "Late" submissions than those with more.
    🔗
    The Deadline marks when the assignment can no longer be submitted in Canvas at all.
    🔗
    Note that some assignments must be submitted before other assignments may be attempted. So watch out - an assignment without a Deadline may be blocking another assignment that does have a Deadline.
    🔗
    🔗
